# Study Guide

## Recommended cadence

Use 60–90 minute sessions. A productive session should have one concrete output: a passing exercise, an experiment result, a diagram, or a written explanation.

A sustainable weekly rhythm is:

- two concept-and-code sessions;
- one experiment session;
- one checkpoint or project session.

At that pace, foundational phases take several weeks and the full curriculum takes several months. Moving faster is fine, but do not skip experiments to manufacture progress.

## The three-pass approach

### Pass 1 — Predict

Before running code, predict:

- its output shape;
- whether loss rises or falls;
- which parameter changes;
- what failure you expect from an invalid configuration.

### Pass 2 — Observe

Run the code. Capture actual outputs and compare them with the prediction. Unexpected results are learning opportunities, not annoyances to scroll past.

### Pass 3 — Explain

Explain the result twice:

1. in plain language for a business or architecture audience;
2. technically for an ML engineer.

## When to move on

Move to the next lesson when:

- the lab runs without copying unexplained commands;
- exercises pass;
- checkpoint answers are in your own words;
- you can identify at least one relevant failure mode;
- you understand how the lesson connects to the next phase.

Do not require perfect mathematical fluency before progressing. Revisit the math when a later implementation gives it a concrete purpose.

## Getting help

When something fails, preserve:

- the command;
- the complete error;
- Python and dependency versions;
- the relevant shapes and input values;
- what you expected.

That information makes debugging scientific rather than ceremonial.
