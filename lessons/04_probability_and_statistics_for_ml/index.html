01

Probability describes possibilities; statistics learns from observations

A probability is a number from 0 to 1 describing uncertainty under stated assumptions. Zero means impossible in the model, one means certain, and values between describe relative uncertainty. Statistics uses observed data to estimate patterns, variation, and uncertainty about a larger process.

PROBABILITY

Model → possible data

If a support ticket has a 20% escalation probability, what outcomes should we expect across many similar tickets?

STATISTICS

Observed data → estimated model

After observing 200 tickets, what escalation rate and uncertainty should we infer?

Metaphor: a weather forecast

A 70% rain forecast does not promise rain on your street. It describes a class of situations: across many appropriately comparable forecasts labeled 70%, rain should occur about 70% of the time.

Where the metaphor stops: probabilities depend on the model, available evidence, reference class, and assumptions. Different forecasting systems may produce different estimates.

Probability is conditional on information. “The chance is 20%” is incomplete unless we know for whom, under what conditions, using which evidence, and for what time horizon.

READINESS CHECK

A forecast says there is a 70% chance of rain. It stays dry today. Was the forecast necessarily wrong?

02

A distribution describes possible values and their probabilities

A probability distribution is a structured description of uncertainty. For a discrete variable, it lists possible values and their probabilities. For a continuous variable, it describes probability density across ranges.

Metaphor: inventory spread across shelves

Imagine probability as one unit of inventory distributed across labeled outcome shelves. More inventory on a shelf means that outcome is more likely. All shelves together must contain the full unit.

Where the metaphor stops: continuous distributions assign probability to ranges, and individual exact points may have zero probability even when nearby ranges are likely.

Expected value is a long-run weighted center

Multiply every outcome by its probability and add the results. It is the average you would approach across many repetitions—not a promise about the next case and not necessarily an outcome that can occur.

expected value = Σ(outcome × probability)

Variance measures spread around the expected value

Two processes can have the same expected value but very different reliability. Variance averages squared distance from the mean; standard deviation is the square root of variance and returns to the original units.

03

A sample is evidence about a population—not the population itself

Population

The full process or group we want to understand.

All future delivery times.

Sample

The observations we actually collected.

Delivery times from 100 orders.

Statistic

A number calculated from the sample.

Sample mean of 49.2 hours.
Metaphor: tasting soup

A spoonful can estimate the whole pot only if the soup was mixed and the spoonful is representative. A larger spoon does not fix sampling from an unmixed pocket of salt.

Where the metaphor stops: real data may change over time, observations may be dependent, and the target population may be poorly defined.

04

Bayes’ rule updates an existing belief with new evidence

When evidence arrives, the updated probability depends on both how diagnostic that evidence is and how common the event was beforehand. That starting frequency is the base rate or prior probability.

Metaphor: sorting mail with a spam filter

A warning label matters differently when spam is common versus rare. Even a good filter can produce many false alarms when nearly all messages are legitimate.

Where the metaphor stops: real systems may have changing base rates, correlated signals, uncertain estimates, and asymmetric costs.

Use counts before formulas

Imagine 10,000 manufactured components. A sensor detects 95% of real defects but falsely flags 5% of good components. If only 1% are actually defective:

  • 100 are defective; about 95 are correctly flagged.
  • 9,900 are good; about 495 are falsely flagged.
  • 590 are flagged in total, but only 95 are truly defective.

A flagged component therefore has only about a 16% chance of truly being defective despite the sensor's 95% sensitivity.

Go deeper: the compact Bayes formula

Posterior probability equals likelihood times prior, divided by the overall probability of the evidence.

P(A | B) = P(B | A) × P(A) ÷ P(B)

P(A | B) means probability of A after observing B. The vertical bar reads “given.” In the component example, A is “defective” and B is “sensor flagged.”

05

A confidence interval describes an estimation procedure

A point estimate such as “the mean is 49.2” hides sampling uncertainty. An interval gives a range produced by a method designed to capture the unknown population quantity at a stated long-run rate.

Metaphor: casting nets repeatedly

Imagine repeating the entire sampling process and casting one interval-shaped net each time. A 95% confidence procedure is designed so about 95 of 100 such nets capture the fixed population value.

Where the metaphor stops: after one frequentist interval is calculated, the parameter is treated as fixed. We do not say there is a 95% probability that this particular interval contains it.

Approximate interval for a mean

Suppose a random sample has mean 50, standard deviation 10, and size 100. The standard error is 10 ÷ √100 = 1. An approximate 95% margin is 1.96 × 1 = 1.96.

50 ± 1.96 → approximately [48.04, 51.96]

Larger representative samples reduce standard error roughly with the square root of sample size. Getting an interval half as wide usually requires about four times as many observations.

What it does say

The procedure has 95% long-run coverage under its assumptions.

What it does not say

95% of individual values fall inside, or the fixed parameter has a 95% frequentist probability of being inside this realized interval.

Intervals inherit data problems. Narrow intervals can be misleading when sampling is biased, observations are dependent, the process drifted, or the formula's assumptions do not fit.

06

Calibration asks whether probabilities match observed frequencies

If a model assigns 80% probability to 100 comparable events, a calibrated model should be correct on about 80 of them over time. Calibration evaluates groups of predictions—not whether one individual prediction “felt confident.”

Metaphor: honest confidence labels

A forecaster who says 80% should be right about eight times in ten across many 80% forecasts. A forecaster who is right only five times in ten is overconfident.

Where the metaphor stops: calibration can differ across probability ranges, time periods, and subgroups, and it does not measure discrimination or usefulness by itself.

07

Correlation is a clue, not proof of causation

Two variables are correlated when they change together. That relationship may be useful for prediction, but it does not prove that changing one variable will change the other.

Hot weather→More ice-cream sales
Hot weather→More swimming→More drownings

Ice-cream sales and drownings may rise together because temperature influences both. Banning ice cream would not address the causal mechanism.

Confounding

A third variable influences both observed variables.

Reverse causation

The supposed outcome may actually influence the supposed cause.

Selection effects

How data enters the sample creates an apparent relationship.

Predictive models can use non-causal correlations. That may be acceptable for stable prediction, but interventions, policy, and changing environments require causal reasoning and stronger evidence.

Engineer deep dive · estimators, scale, and failure modes

Prerequisites: Complete the core interactions first.

A sample statistic is an estimator: a rule mapping observed data to an unknown population quantity. Separate random error, which repeated sampling may reduce, from systematic bias, which more rows may only measure more precisely. Confidence intervals depend on the sampling process; calibration depends on comparable future cases; Bayesian updates depend on a defensible prior and likelihood.

At production scale, measure calibration by probability band and operational slice, attach sample counts to estimates, and alert on population or label shift. A narrow interval remains untrustworthy when records are dependent, selected, delayed, or mislabeled.

Architecture review question: What evidence would show that this model's 80% risk scores still mean 80% after the customer mix changes?
